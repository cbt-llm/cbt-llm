Where do LLMs fall short in CBT-guided reasoning?

A fluent, compassionate answer can still miss the thinking behind a person’s distress. CBT Lens explores how models understand that thinking, choose a strategy, and respond.

Start with the thought beneath the words.

The research question: Can a model identify the user’s cognitive context and choose an appropriate CBT strategy, rather than defaulting to the same reassuring pattern?

03 / The approach

From narrative to grounded strategy.

The paper links cognitive conceptualization, clinical concepts, and multiple reasoning paths before a response strategy is selected.

01 — CONCEPTUALIZE

Read the cognitive context

Break a user narrative into elements of Beck’s cognitive conceptualization framework.

02 — GROUND

Check relevant concepts

Connect the context to SNOMED CT concepts and validate their relevance with natural language inference.

03 — SELECT

Choose a CBT strategy

Use multiple chains of thought to select validation and reflection, Socratic questioning, or an alternative perspective.

04 / Protocol Leverage Force

How much leverage did the CBT protocol have?

Protocol Leverage Force (F) measures how strongly CBT guidance reorients a response away from the model’s baseline and toward the user’s utterance. It is a behavioral measure, not a score of therapeutic quality.

wprotocol

How confidently the framework selected a CBT protocol for this turn.

Δ · shift toward the user

Positive Δ means the guided response moved closer to the user’s utterance than the baseline did. The formula squares its magnitude, so the sign must be read separately.

Distance from baseline

Compares the guided response with the baseline response centroid. ε reflects how much the baseline itself changes across prompt paraphrases.

Across three open-weight models and 14 RealCBT-derived case studies, models often favored validation and reflection. The metric helps identify how guidance changed an answer; expert review is still needed to assess the answer itself.

05 / The affective story

What happens to emotion across a conversation?

The paper compares valence (the tone of user language) and arousal (its intensity) across CBT-MCoT synthetic sessions and RealCBT human transcripts. Each turn updates a cumulative mean scored with the NRC VAD Lexicon; the plotted paths summarize 14 transcripts per model.

Human conversations move more. The longer RealCBT transcripts show more turns and more oscillation in affective language.

Synthetic paths look smoother. The CBT-MCoT sessions trace shorter, less varied trajectories in the paper’s plots.

A trace is context, not an outcome. Valence and arousal describe language over time; neither shows whether a person improved clinically.
